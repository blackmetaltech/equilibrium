## 1. Bhopal, India, 1984
- **When and where:** night of 2-3 December 1984, Union Carbide India Ltd pesticide plant, Bhopal.
- **What the plant made:** carbaryl. Methyl isocyanate (MIC), a toxic intermediate, was stored in large tanks.

**What happened.** Water is believed to have entered a tank holding about 40 t of MIC. The reaction that followed heated and pressurised the tank, and released more than 40 t of toxic gas over the neighbourhoods beside the plant. Several safeguards were not working at the time:
- the MIC refrigeration unit had been drained of coolant;
- the vent-gas scrubber had been switched off;
- the flare tower had been out of action for about three months.

Maintenance and staffing had been reduced. There was no effective warning or emergency plan for the people living nearby. How the water got in is disputed: Union Carbide says sabotage, and independent sources dispute this.

**Toll (attributed).**
- Broughton (2005) review: at least 3,800 immediate deaths, with early estimates up to 10,000.
- Broughton (2005) review: 15,000-20,000 premature deaths over the next two decades, probably an undercount.
- Madhya Pradesh government: 3,787 gas-related deaths **[UNVERIFIED]**.
- 2010 curative petition: 5,295 deaths and about 527,894 injured **[UNVERIFIED]**.
- Survivor groups: say the true toll is several times the official figures.

**Root causes.**
- Several safeguards were unavailable at the same time.
- The inventory of a very toxic material was large.
- Maintenance and staffing had been reduced.
- There was no community emergency plan.

**Lesson (game mechanic).** Safeguards only protect people if they are kept working, and the less hazardous material you store, the less there is to go wrong. This maps to interlocks, maintenance and inventory.

**What changed.**
- Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster Act 1985.
- Environment (Protection) Act 1986.
- Public Liability Insurance Act 1991 **[UNVERIFIED source]**.
- 1989 settlement of US$470 M.
- A driver of process-safety-management and community right-to-know laws worldwide **[UNVERIFIED source]**.

**Sources.**
- Broughton E., "The Bhopal disaster and its aftermath: a review", *Environmental Health* 4:6 (2005), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1142333/ (FETCHED).
- Bhopal Medical Appeal, curative petition page (survivor perspective).

## 2. Seveso, Italy, 1976
- **When and where:** 10 July 1976, ICMESA plant, Meda, near Milan. The cloud reached Seveso and nearby towns.
- **What the plant made:** 2,4,5-trichlorophenol.

**What happened.** A batch was stopped part-way for the weekend with the mixture still in the reactor. Residual steam heat took it past the temperature at which a runaway begins, about 180 °C, roughly 50 °C below what operators believed (Kletz, as cited). The relief system vented a cloud containing the dioxin TCDD straight to the atmosphere, and it settled over about 18 km². Warnings to residents were slow, and evacuation of the worst zone began about 15 days later.

**Toll.**
- No immediate human deaths reported.
- Chloracne in about 193 people early on, about 640 in total.
- Long-term epidemiological follow-up continues (Bertazzi, Pesatori, Eskenazi).
- About 3,300 animals died and more than 80,000 were culled.
- Estimates of the TCDD released vary (about 1 kg to 15-30 kg, disputed).

**Root causes.**
- The runaway pathway was not characterised.
- The reactor was left in an uncontrolled state.
- There was no instrument reading the heating-steam temperature.
- The relief discharged to the atmosphere with no containment.
- Emergency communication was slow.

**Lesson (game mechanic).** Know the temperature at which your reaction runs away, and never leave a reactor in an uncontrolled state. A relief system must send material somewhere safe. This maps to Semenov, relief and interlocks.

**What changed.** The EU Seveso Directive 82/501/EEC (1982), later Seveso II (96/82/EC) and Seveso III (2012/18/EU). The day and month and the EUR-Lex text are **[UNVERIFIED]**.

**Sources.**
- Wikipedia "Seveso disaster", which cites Kletz, Eskenazi et al. and Bertazzi et al. (FETCHED; replace with the primary citations).
- EU MAHB bulletin no. 9.
- EUR-Lex 31982L0501.

## 3. T2 Laboratories, Jacksonville, Florida, USA, 2007
- **When and where:** 19 December 2007, 13:33.
- **What the plant made:** methylcyclopentadienyl manganese tricarbonyl (MCMT), a gasoline additive, made in a batch reactor.

**What happened.** During the 175th batch the reactor lost cooling. The CSB concluded this was likely a blockage in the water supply or a valve failure. The exothermic reaction accelerated, and the temperature and pressure rose out of control. The rupture disc opened, but it was far too small for a runaway, and the reactor burst.

**Toll (CSB).** Four people died and 32 were injured: 4 employees and 28 members of the public in nearby businesses.

**Root causes (CSB).**
- Root cause: T2 did not recognise the runaway hazard of its process.
- Contributing: the cooling system had single-point failures and no redundancy.
- Contributing: the relief system could not handle a runaway.

**Lesson (game mechanic).** An exothermic reactor needs cooling that no single fault can take away, and relief sized for the runaway case, not normal operation. This maps to L24 runaway and L27 relief sizing.

**What changed (CSB).** Recommendations to AIChE and ABET to add reactive-hazard awareness to chemical engineering degrees (2008-03-I-FL-1 and -3), closed as "Exceeds Recommended Action". The CSB also published a safety video, "Runaway".

**Sources.**
- CSB investigation page, https://www.csb.gov/t2-laboratories-inc-reactive-chemical-explosion/ (FETCHED).
- CSB final report, September 2009 (open before release).

## 4. LG Polymers, Visakhapatnam, India, 2020
- **When and where:** early morning of 7 May 2020, R. R. Venkatapuram.
- **What the plant made:** polystyrene. Styrene monomer was stored on site. The product range and the LG Chem parent are **[UNVERIFIED]**.

**What happened.** The plant was restarting after weeks of lockdown. Styrene in a storage tank heated, which is consistent with polymerisation, vaporised and escaped through the tank vents into villages where people were asleep. Investigators reported these problems:
- temperature was measured only at the bottom of the tank;
- the rising temperature went unnoticed;
- after a design change, the inhibitor may not have reached the upper liquid;
- the site alarm was not used;
- warnings to residents failed.

**Toll.** 12 deaths (Andhra Pradesh High Powered Committee, as reported by PTI). Hundreds were hospitalised **[UNVERIFIED count]**.

**Root causes (as reported).**
- NGT Joint Monitoring Committee: "gross human failure" and lack of experience with idle full styrene tanks.
- State High Powered Committee: management responsible for 20 of 21 major causes.

**Lesson (game mechanic).** A stored monomer must be kept cool and inhibited, you must be able to see the whole tank's temperature, and the alarms must work. This maps to monitoring, alarms and runaway.

**What changed.** An NGT interim deposit order, arrests of officials, and recommendations to relocate the plant. Later court outcomes and regulation are **[UNVERIFIED]**.

- Deccan Herald / PTI on the HPC findings;
- Business Today on the NGT JMC report;
- India Environment Portal copy of the JMC report of 28 May 2020 (likely the primary source).
